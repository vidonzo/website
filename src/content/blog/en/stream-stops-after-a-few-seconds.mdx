Buffering has a look: the wheel, the stall, the picture catching up. This is
not that. This is a channel that opens cleanly, plays perfectly for five or ten
or thirty seconds, and then stops — back to the list, or a frozen frame, or an
error, or the same channel starting over from the beginning. If you press play
it happens again. It is a *disconnect*, and the causes are almost entirely
different from [the ones behind buffering](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/). Here
are the five that account for nearly all of them, and what a player can and
cannot do about each.

## 1. The server ended the connection

The commonest cause. A provider's server hands you a stream and, some seconds
later, closes the connection — because it is overloaded, because a process
restarted, because a load balancer moved you, because it is Tuesday. On a raw
transport stream the data simply stops arriving; on
[HLS](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/) the next segment never comes.

This is the case a player is *for*. A well-built one does not treat a dropped
connection as the end of the programme; it treats it as a hiccup and quietly
reconnects. Vidonzo does this at the transport layer: a stalled read times out
within seconds rather than hanging, and the connection is re-established with
a short, bounded back-off, so a server that closed the door reopens it without
you seeing more than a moment's pause. If a channel used to die at ten seconds
in your old player and merely blinks in this one, that mechanism is why.

The signature: random channels, random times, more in the evening. Nothing to
change on your side; if it happens on *every* channel constantly, the server is
in genuine trouble and the provider needs to hear it.

## 2. Your access expired mid-stream

Xtream Codes and many M3U links carry a token — a proof of who you are — in
the address. Tokens expire. When one does, the server does not close the
connection politely; it answers the next request with an error: forbidden,
unauthorised, gone.

Here a good player deliberately does *not* keep reconnecting. Hammering a
server that has just said "no" is pointless and, in volume, looks like an
attack; providers block for it. Vidonzo reconnects through a dropped
connection and stops at an explicit refusal, on purpose. The cure is a fresh
playlist: use *Refresh now* on the source so the player re-reads the current
list — or, if the subscription itself lapsed, that is between you and the
provider.

The signature: it worked yesterday, everything fails today, and the failure is
instant rather than a few seconds in. See also
[the five faults behind a link that will not load](/blog/playlist-link-wont-load/).

## 3. Too many streams at once

Most subscriptions allow a fixed number of *simultaneous* streams — one, two,
sometimes more. Open a third and the server drops one, and it is not always the
newest. The dropped one looks exactly like case 1, except that it correlates
with someone else in the house pressing play.

The signature: it fails when another device is watching, and recovers when
that one stops. This is not a player fault and there is no setting for it;
it is the arrangement you have with the provider, and the fix is either
watching in turns or a plan that allows more. A player that shows a clear
error rather than silently reopening the same stream at least tells you which
case you are in.

## 4. The device went to sleep on the connection

Phones and TV boxes save power aggressively, and a video stream is exactly the
kind of long, quiet connection they like to cut. On a phone this shows as a
stream that dies when the screen dims or when you switch briefly to another
app; on a TV box it can be an over-eager Wi-Fi power-saving mode that drops
the radio between segments.

The signature: tied to *your* actions — the screen, the app switch, a pause —
not to the clock or the channel. On a phone, keep the app in the foreground
while playing; Vidonzo gates its recovery to the foreground on purpose, so it
does not fight the operating system for a connection you have walked away
from. On a box, look for a Wi-Fi power-saving or "keep awake" setting, or
[move the box to a wire](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/).

## 5. The network in between dropped it

Between the server and the screen sit your router, your ISP, and — for some
people — a VPN or a proxy. Any of them can reset a connection: a router
rotating a NAT table, an ISP's middlebox timing out an idle-looking flow, a
VPN reconnecting. The player sees the same thing it sees in case 1, and it
reconnects the same way; the difference is that *everything* is affected, not
just one provider.

The signature: other streaming apps drop too, and the pattern follows the
network — a particular router, a particular VPN, a particular time of day.
Test on mobile data for a minute; if the drops vanish, the fault is on the
home network side.

## Telling them apart in a minute

- Random channels, random times, brief pause → the server dropped it (1). The
  player reconnects; nothing to do.
- Instant failure on everything, worked yesterday → expired access (2).
  *Refresh now*, then talk to the provider.
- Fails when someone else presses play → too many streams (3).
- Dies when you dim the screen or switch apps → power saving (4).
- Everything drops, other apps too → the network (5).

## What the player owes you

The honest boundary is this: a player should reconnect through *every* drop
that is not a refusal, fast enough that you rarely notice, and it should stop
cleanly and say so at every drop that is. Anything more — keeping a stream
open past its token, ignoring a stream limit — is not a feature, it is a
problem with a delay. Get the reconnecting right and case 1, which is most of
the cases, stops being a thing you think about.
