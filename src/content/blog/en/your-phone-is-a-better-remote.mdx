A d-pad has four directions and an OK button. A typical IPTV playlist has twenty
thousand channels. These two facts do not get along.

You can paper over the gap with better focus handling and faster scrolling — and
Vidonzo does both — but the underlying problem is that you are driving a database
query with an arrow key. Meanwhile there is a device on the sofa arm with a
touchscreen, a keyboard, and a search field. Using it as the remote is not a
convenience bolted on the side. It is the only input in the room that matches the
size of the list.

## What the remote actually does

Vidonzo's remote is not a virtual d-pad drawn on a phone. Those exist, and they
are worse than the physical remote they imitate: you lose the raised edges that
let you press up twice without looking, and you gain nothing, because the
interface you are driving is still the one on the television.

Instead, the phone gets the **library**. You browse the television's full
catalogue on the phone, with the phone's scrolling and the phone's keyboard.
When you find something, one tap starts it on the television. The screen never
shows a menu you are navigating — it shows what is playing.

That inverts the usual arrangement. The big screen is for watching; the small
screen is for deciding. It also means the room is not held hostage by one
person's search: what is on the television keeps running while somebody looks for
what comes after it.

You also get a queue, so choosing what is next does not interrupt what is on,
favourites you can toggle from either device, and a trackpad and keyboard for
the rare moments a TV app really does need text entry.

Searching from the phone is the change most visible in daily use, particularly
with a provider that prefixes channel names heavily —
[which is what makes a long list reachable at all](/blog/organize-large-playlists/).

## Over your network, not through ours

Pairing runs over the local network. The phone finds the television by mDNS —
Vidonzo announces itself, so there are no codes to type in the normal case — and
the two talk over a WebSocket on your own LAN.

Nothing about the connection needs our servers, and that has two consequences
worth stating separately. It works when your internet connection is down, because
neither the control channel nor a stream from a local source has any reason to
leave the house. And we cannot see what you browse, because there is no point in
the path where we appear — the same reasoning that runs through
[the bring-your-own-playlist model](/blog/bring-your-own-playlist/).

Some routers block discovery between wireless clients, which stops mDNS reaching
anything. For those, there is an explicit path that does not depend on discovery
at all: a QR code, with a short code to type where scanning is awkward.

## The watch

A paired smartwatch gets play, pause, skip and volume. It is a deliberately small
surface — enough to stop the episode when the doorbell goes, without pretending a
watch is a good place to browse a catalogue. Anything more would make it a worse
copy of the phone that is already in the room.

## What this costs

A local-network feature is harder to support than a cloud one. Guest networks,
client isolation, and mDNS being blocked all produce the same symptom — the TV
does not appear — and none of them are visible from our side. We find out when
somebody writes to us.

A relay through a server would make discovery someone else's problem. It would
also mean every button you press travels through us, which is precisely the
property we did not want. So the trade stands: the traffic stays on your network,
and the support burden stays with us.

That is what the QR path is for, and it is why
[the pairing instructions](/help/) lead with the network questions rather than
the app ones.
