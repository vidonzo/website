Most playback failures are silent in both senses — nothing plays, and nothing
explains why. This one is different, and the difference is a gift: you can hear
the commentary perfectly while the screen stays black. Something is arriving,
something is being understood, and exactly one stage of the chain has given up.

That narrows the field considerably. Your playlist is fine — the stream would
not have opened otherwise. The server is fine, and so is
[the path between you and it](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/): audio is a live
demonstration that bytes are still landing. What is left is the last stage
before the picture, the one that turns compressed data back into frames.

## Codecs and containers are not the same thing

The `.m3u8` at the end of a stream URL tells you almost nothing about this.
That is the *container* — the envelope that carries the tracks and the timing
that keeps them together. Whether your device can play what is inside is a
separate question, decided by the **codec**: the compression scheme each track
was encoded with.

A live stream typically braids at least two of them —
[video, audio, and sometimes text](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/) — and
each is compressed by its own scheme. Two identical-looking streams from the
same provider can therefore behave completely differently on the same device.

## Why the picture fails and the sound does not

Audio compression is cheap. Any processor made this century can decode a stereo
audio track in software without noticing the effort, so audio almost always
just works. Video is the opposite: decoding a 1080p picture in real time is
genuinely expensive, and modern schemes buy their smaller file sizes with more
arithmetic per frame.

That expense is why devices contain a dedicated **decoder chip**, and why the
chip is where compatibility goes to die. It implements a *fixed list* of
formats, frozen when the hardware was designed. H.264 is on every list. H.265 —
also called HEVC, and increasingly common because it carries the same picture
in noticeably less bandwidth — is on most modern ones and missing from plenty
of older boxes. Newer schemes such as AV1 are missing from most.

When a stream arrives in a format the chip does not implement, the audio track
sails through and the video track stops dead. Hence the black screen with
perfect sound.

There is a subtler variant worth knowing about, because it explains the cases
where one HEVC channel plays and the next one does not. A chip's support is
never simply "HEVC" — it covers a particular *profile* and *level*: a colour
depth, a resolution ceiling, a frame-rate ceiling. A 10-bit stream on an 8-bit
decoder, or 4K on a chip specified to 1080p, is refused just as flatly as an
unknown format. And some chips accept a stream, report success, and then quietly
produce nothing at all.

## The fix that should not be your job

There is always a fallback: the general-purpose processor can decode any format
in software, given enough cycles. It costs battery, it makes the device warm,
and on a weak box it may not keep up with a high-resolution stream at all. But
it works when the chip will not.

So the fix is known. The problem is that the *diagnosis* has historically been
pushed onto the viewer, in the form of a decoder setting in a menu — a choice
that asks you to know something you have no way of knowing, about a chip you
have never seen a specification for, while a black screen sits in front of you.

Vidonzo treats that as the player's job instead. Hardware decoding is on by
default, because it is the right answer nearly always: it is faster and it uses
far less power. What matters is what happens when it is the wrong answer.
The player watches two things at once — whether bytes are arriving, and whether
any frame has been produced. Those two facts together are unambiguous. Data
with no picture is a decoder problem and nothing else; no data at all is a
network problem wearing the same black screen, and the decoder is innocent.

When the first pattern holds for several seconds, the player steps down to
software decoding and reopens the stream by itself. No dialog, no question, no
menu — the loading state simply says it is still adjusting. If a second stream
needs the same rescue, the session stops re-learning the lesson on every channel
change and starts lower. That memory is deliberately *not* permanent: one
awkward stream should not condemn your GPU forever, so a fresh start begins at
the top of the ladder again and earns its way down only if it must.

The switch in **Settings → Playback → Hardware decoding** stays, for the rare
device where the chip accepts everything and renders nothing. It is not a
setting you should ever need to reach for.

## Reading the evidence yourself

The info button in the player controls opens **Stream info**, which is the
short version of everything above:

- **Codec** — what the video and audio tracks actually are. If this reads HEVC
  on a box from several years ago, you have your explanation.
- **Decoder** — which path is doing the work right now. Seeing software here
  means the ladder already rescued this stream.
- **Resolution and frame rate** — the numbers that decide whether software
  decoding can plausibly keep up.
- **Bitrate and buffer** — the network side, for
  [telling a decode failure apart from a starving connection](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/).

Software decoding at 1080p is fine on a modern phone and marginal on a cheap TV
stick. If the picture is present but juddering and the decoder reads software,
you are watching a processor lose a race — the failure has simply moved one step
later.

## The mirror-image symptom

Picture with no sound is the same story told the other way round. Multichannel
audio formats are the usual cause: a stream carrying surround audio on a device
that only handles stereo, or a television passing audio through to a sound bar
that does not accept that format. The fix is usually a different audio track,
where the stream offers one — the same
[track selector](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/) that switches languages.

## When it is not a codec at all

Two failures look similar and are not:

- **Black screen and no sound, ever.** Nothing decoded because nothing arrived.
  That is [the stream or the path to it](/blog/playlist-link-wont-load/), not
  the decoder — and if it only happens in certain rooms,
  [the radio is the suspect](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/).
- **Everything plays, but the list is unreadable.** Text, not video —
  [a character-encoding problem in the playlist file](/blog/why-channel-names-look-garbled/).

A decode failure is specific: a stream that opens, a buffer that fills, audio
that plays, and a picture that never appears.

## What this means for choosing a device

If one particular stream fails on your TV box and plays on your phone, the
stream is fine and the box is old. That is worth knowing before you spend an
evening on settings — hardware decoders cannot be updated, because they are
silicon, not software.

It is also the strongest argument for a player that adapts silently rather than
one that hands you a decoder menu. [On a television](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/),
where the whole interface is driven by a d-pad from three metres away, a modal
question about video decoding is not a feature. The player's job is
[to read your list, fetch the stream and draw the picture](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/) —
including when the obvious way to draw it turns out not to work.
