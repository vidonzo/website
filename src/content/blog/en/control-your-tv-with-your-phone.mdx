**There are three ways to control a TV with your phone: the remote app made for
your TV's platform, which works over Wi-Fi; an infrared blaster, which some
phones have built in; and an app on the TV that pairs with its own companion on
the phone.** They are not interchangeable. The first two replace the remote for
the whole television. The third controls one app, and does it far better than a
remote can. Which one you want depends on whether you are trying to change the
volume or find a programme.

## 1. The remote app for your TV's platform

Every major smart-TV platform has an official phone app with a remote built in:

- **Google TV and Android TV** — the Google TV app, which on many Android phones
  also puts a remote tile in the Quick Settings panel.
- **Samsung** — the SmartThings app.
- **LG** — the LG ThinQ app.
- **Apple TV** — the Remote built into Control Center on the iPhone.
- **Fire TV** — Amazon's Fire TV app.
- **Roku** — the Roku app.

**How to pair:** connect the phone to the **same Wi-Fi network** as the TV, open
the app, and choose your television from the list it finds. The first time, the
TV shows a code that you type on the phone. After that it reconnects on its own.

**What you get:** a d-pad, back and home buttons, volume, often a keyboard for
search fields, and sometimes power. On most platforms the keyboard is the real
win — typing a password or a film title with a phone is a different experience
from pecking at letters with arrow keys.

**Its limits:** the TV must be on the network. Turning a TV *on* from a phone
only works if the set keeps its network connection in standby, which is often a
setting you have to enable and costs a little power.

## 2. An infrared blaster

Some Android phones — Xiaomi models most commonly — have a small infrared
emitter on the top edge, the same kind of light an ordinary remote uses. With the
phone maker's remote app you pick your TV brand and the phone becomes a
conventional remote.

**What you get:** power, volume, channels and inputs on almost any television,
including older sets with no network at all. No pairing, no Wi-Fi.

**Its limits:** you have to point it at the TV, there is no keyboard, and iPhones
do not have the hardware. If your phone lacks an emitter, no app can add one.

## 3. An app that pairs with its own app on the TV

Some TV apps come with a phone companion that controls that one app only. It
cannot change your TV's input or switch it off, but inside the app it does things
a general remote cannot, because it knows what is on the screen.

Vidonzo works this way on Android TV and Google TV:

- **Pairing:** the TV shows a QR code — **Scan to connect** — and a six-digit
  code that renews itself. Scan it or enter the code from Vidonzo on your phone
  or tablet, then approve the device on the TV. You choose there whether it gets playback
  controls only, or full control including browsing.
- **What the phone becomes:** a d-pad and a touchpad, a keyboard whose text
  lands in the TV's search field, and — the part that matters with a big
  playlist — the TV's library itself: search, favourites, continue watching,
  live channels and a queue, browsed on the phone and started on the TV with one
  tap.
- **Where it runs:** over your own network, between your devices.
  [Why it is built that way, and what it costs](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/).

It controls Vidonzo, not the television. The volume it changes is the player's.

## Which one should you use?

- **To switch the TV on, change the input or set the volume**, use the TV
  maker's app, or an infrared blaster if your phone has one.
- **To type**, any of the Wi-Fi options beats the remote, and the platform's app
  is the one that works everywhere on the TV.
- **To find something in a very long channel list**, a remote that
  controls the app itself is the only one that shows you the list. A
  [player built for the remote](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) makes a d-pad
  bearable; a phone makes it unnecessary.

Many people end up with two: the TV maker's app for the television, and the
in-app remote for watching.

## Controlling is not the same as casting

"Connecting a phone to a TV" can also mean **casting** or **mirroring** — Google
Cast, AirPlay or screen mirroring — where the phone's picture or a video from it
appears on the TV. That sends content to the screen; it does not steer what the
TV is doing. If what you want is a remote, the three options above are the ones
to look at.

## When the phone cannot find the TV

The Wi-Fi methods all discover the TV on your local network, so the same few
things block every one of them:

- **Different networks.** A phone on the 5 GHz band and a TV on 2.4 GHz are fine
  if they are the same network; a phone on the guest network is not. Guest
  networks usually hide devices from each other on purpose.
- **Client isolation.** Some routers and mesh systems have a setting that stops
  devices on the same network from seeing each other. Pairing needs it off.
- **A VPN on the phone** can route its traffic away from your local network.
- **A weak signal where the TV stands**, which tends to show up in
  [playback too](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/).

Put both on the same main network, try again, and the TV appears.
