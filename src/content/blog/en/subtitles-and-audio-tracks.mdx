The thing you watch as "a channel" is really several streams braided together:
one video track, one or more audio tracks, and — sometimes — one or more text
tracks. The braid is assembled by your provider before it ever reaches you, and
that one fact explains nearly everything confusing about subtitles and
languages in IPTV: a player can pick apart the braid and let you choose between
strands, but it cannot add a strand that was never woven in.

## Tracks travel inside the stream

When a broadcaster sends a film with two languages and subtitles, all of it
rides in the same transport stream: the video, an audio track per language,
and the subtitle data, each tagged with a language code. A player's job is to
read those tags, present the choices, and switch between them —
[decode and draw, in other words](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/), applied to
more than just the picture.

So the first diagnostic question is always the same: *is the track in the
stream at all?* If the player's audio menu lists one track and no subtitles,
that is not the player being coy. The provider's braid contains one strand of
audio and nothing else, and no setting anywhere will conjure the rest.

## Why subtitles go missing more than audio does

Subtitles are the most frequently dropped strand, for unglamorous technical
reasons. Broadcast subtitles come in awkward shapes — some are transmitted as
*pictures* of text rather than text itself — and when a provider re-encodes a
channel [into a leaner delivery format](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/),
carrying those along is extra work that is easy to skip. The channel you watch
may have had subtitles when it left the broadcaster and lost them in
transit.

The practical test: if the same programme shows subtitles on one source and
none on another, the strand was dropped upstream. Mention it to your provider —
it is genuinely fixable at their end, and only at their end.

## Audio tracks, and the mystery of "und"

Where a stream does carry several audio tracks — original language plus a dub,
or commentary in two languages — switching between them is a player menu away.
The common irritation is labelling: language tags are set by whoever encoded
the stream, and a lazy encoder leaves them as `und` — undetermined. The player
can only show the tag it was given, so "Track 1 (und)" and "Track 2 (und)"
means trying both, once, and remembering which was which.

If you watch in more than one language, this is one half of a bilingual setup —
[the other half is the app itself speaking your language](/blog/watch-in-your-language/),
interface and content being separate choices.

## Bringing your own subtitle file

For films and series, there is a healthy world of subtitle files that exist
separately from any stream. A capable player will let you attach one — point it
at a subtitle file and it is rendered over the video as if it had been in the
braid all along. It is the one case where a missing strand can be supplied
from your side, and for older films in particular it is often the only way to
watch with text at all.

## Making them readable where you sit

Rendering subtitles is the player's craft, and it is not one-size-fits-all.
Text comfortable from three metres away on a television is a billboard
[on a tablet at arm's length](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/); thin white text is
invisible over a snowy football pitch. A player worth keeping lets you set the
size, the text colour, and the opacity of the backing strip behind the text —
three plain controls that between them solve almost every legibility
complaint, from sofa distance to bright-room glare.

## The short diagnostic, in order

1. **Open the player's track menus.** What the menus list is what the braid
   contains. If the strand is absent, stop — the fix is upstream.
2. **Tracks listed but wrong by default?** Choose the one you want; a good
   player keeps your preference.
3. **Labelled `und`?** Try each track once; the labels were left blank at the
   encoder.
4. **Subtitles present but unreadable?** Size, colour, background — adjust
   where you actually sit.
5. **A film with no text track at all?** A separate subtitle file, attached in
   the player, fills the gap.

None of this requires knowing anything about the braid. But knowing it is a
braid — assembled upstream, unpicked at your end — is the difference between
fiddling with settings that cannot help and asking the one question that can:
was the strand ever there at all?
