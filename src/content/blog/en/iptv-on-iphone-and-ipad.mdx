A playlist does not know what kind of device is reading it. The same Xtream
credentials, the same M3U link and the same channels behave identically on iOS
and on Android, because the format is the format. What differs is everything
*around* the player: how software gets onto the device, how a file gets into the
app, how the chip decodes video, and how the picture reaches a television.

Most of those differences are the platform's, not the app's. Here is which ones
actually change what you do.

## One way in, and it is the App Store

Apple ships no equivalent to sideloading. On Android, a TV box can be handed a
package directly, which is exactly how
[a television build reaches a device](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) that has
no app store worth using. On iOS there is one install path, one review process,
and one build.

Two practical consequences follow. Updates arrive when review says they do,
not when the build finishes. And there is no way to put a player onto an Apple
TV that Apple has not approved for that device — which is why, in a room where
the television is the main screen, a cheap Android box remains the sturdier
answer than an iPhone and a cable.

## Getting a playlist in, without a file manager

There are three ways into a player: Xtream credentials, a remote M3U or M3U8
link, and a playlist file already sitting on the device. On Android the third is
routine — you point the app at a path. On iOS there are no paths to point at.

That is not a limitation so much as a different shape. A local file arrives
through the Files app or iCloud Drive, handed to the player by the system rather
than fetched by it. It works, but it is the fiddliest of the three on this
platform, and it is the one that goes stale: a file copied across once never
updates when the provider changes the list.

On iOS the two remote paths are simply better.
[Xtream credentials or a URL](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/) both re-fetch on their
own, and neither depends on where a file happens to live. If you are
[setting a source up for the first time](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/), start
there.

## The battery is a constraint a TV box does not have

A television box is plugged into a wall and can afford to be inefficient. A
phone cannot, and this is where iOS hardware turns out to matter more than iOS
software.

Apple silicon decodes H.264 and HEVC in a dedicated block of the chip — fast,
and nearly free in power terms. A stream in some other format has to be decoded
by the CPU instead, which works, but you feel it: the phone warms, the battery
drops, and on a long stream the system starts throttling to protect itself.
That is the mechanism behind
[a channel that plays but drains the device](/blog/why-some-channels-dont-play/),
and it is why the same playlist can feel effortless on one channel and
punishing on the next.

Low Power Mode makes this worse in a way that surprises people. It reduces the
clock speeds a software decoder depends on, so a stream that was marginal
becomes unwatchable the moment the battery crosses 20% and the prompt appears.
If playback degrades at a suspiciously round battery percentage, that is why.

## Getting it onto the television

Without a native app on the TV, AirPlay is the bridge — and it is worth being
clear about what it is. Screen mirroring sends the whole display, re-encoded, over
your Wi-Fi, which means it inherits every weakness of
[the radio link](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/) and adds a second encode on top.
It is genuinely useful for showing something to the room for ten minutes. It is a
poor foundation for watching every evening.

For the main screen, the honest recommendation is a device built for the job:
[a television app driven by a remote](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/), with the
phone kept for [what a phone is good at](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/) —
searching, typing and queueing, none of which a d-pad does well.

## The iPad is a different device, not a large iPhone

Screen size changes which interface is correct, not just how big it is. An iPad
has room for a channel list and a playing stream at the same time, which is the
layout a phone cannot offer and a television does not want. Split View puts a
programme guide next to something else entirely. A keyboard case turns search
from a chore into the fastest way to navigate
[a list too long to scroll](/blog/organize-large-playlists/).

It is also the screen that actually gets carried around the house, which is a
larger part of [the tablet's case](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/) than its resolution
ever was.

## Language, and a setting people miss

iOS carries a per-app language setting — Settings, then the app, then Language —
that is independent of the system language. It is genuinely useful if you keep
your phone in one language and prefer to watch in another, and it applies to
right-to-left layout as well, so an app switched to Persian, Arabic or Urdu
flips its layout without the whole device following.

That is separate from [audio and subtitle tracks](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/),
which come from the stream and are chosen per channel. The two are often
confused, and they fail for entirely different reasons.

## What does not change

Almost everything that matters, as it turns out. The playlist formats are the
same. [The programme guide](/blog/epg-explained/) is the same XMLTV or Xtream
data with the same time-zone traps. The
[diagnosis chain for a stalling stream](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) runs in the
same order. And the security model does not soften because the platform has a
reputation for being locked down —
[credentials should be encrypted on the device](/blog/why-a-player-should-encrypt-your-credentials/)
whichever device it is, because the threat was never the operating system.

Vidonzo is on the App Store for iPhone and iPad, and it is the same player
underneath as every other build: the same libmpv engine, the same three ways in,
no advertising SDKs and no behavioural tracking on any platform. The differences
above are iOS being iOS. They are worth knowing, and none of them is a reason to
watch on a smaller screen than you meant to.
