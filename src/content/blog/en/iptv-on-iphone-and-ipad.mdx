IPTV has a reputation as an Android habit, and the reputation has a reason:
Android will run almost anything, and a lot of what it runs is IPTV players.
But the thing you actually own — a playlist from a provider — has no idea what
device it is on. It is a login or a link, and an iPhone opens links. Here is
what is genuinely different on iOS and iPadOS, what is identical, and the two
places where people trip.

## What is identical: the playlist and the player model

Your provider gave you [one of two things](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/): an
Xtream Codes login or an M3U/M3U8 address. Both are just text. Nothing about
either is Android-shaped, and the model of the app is the same on every
platform: [a player reads your list, fetches the stream, draws the picture](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/).
It ships with no channels of its own, and it does not need an account to
start. Paste the same login into Vidonzo on an iPhone and on an Android TV and
you get the same library, because the library was never in the app.

## What is different: how the app arrives

On Android you may have sideloaded an APK. On iOS you will not; the App Store
is the only route for ordinary users, and that is not a limitation to work
around — it is the platform's whole model. Install Vidonzo from the App Store,
open it, add your source. There is no "unknown sources" toggle to find, and
updates arrive by themselves. If a website tells you to install a
configuration profile or a "signed" package to get an IPTV player on an
iPhone, close the tab; nothing about a playlist requires that.

## What is different: where a file lives

Most people use a link, and a link works identically everywhere. If your
provider gave you an M3U *file*, iOS handles it slightly differently from
Android: there is no system-wide file browser outside the Files app. The file
lives there — in iCloud Drive, in "On My iPhone", or in a folder another app
saved it to — and the player asks you to pick it from there. Once picked, it
behaves [like any local file](/blog/playlist-url-vs-local-file/): frozen at
the moment you received it, needing no server, and needing a fresh copy when
the provider changes something. A link stays current on its own; if you have
the choice, prefer the link.

## What is different: the decoder

iPhones and iPads decode video with a hardware block that is very good at the
common formats — H.264 and HEVC — and does nothing at all for the rare ones.
A player on iOS therefore leans on the system decoder for the usual case and
falls back to software for the unusual one. In practice this means the common
streams play cool and long on battery, and an odd stream may warm the phone
more than you expect. If a particular channel [plays sound but no picture](/blog/sound-but-no-picture/),
the article on decoders explains what is happening; it is a stream problem, not
an Apple one.

## What is different: the tablet is not a small TV

An iPad is [the screen that moves](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/) — the kitchen, the
garden, the bed. The interface adapts to it: a denser grid than a TV can
afford, because the screen is close enough to touch, and subtitle size you can
turn down from television scale to caption scale. It is a genuinely different
way of watching, and in a lot of households the tablet ends up used more than
the television.

## What is the same again: the phone as the remote

Here is the part Apple users assume they miss out on. If there is an Android TV
in the house running Vidonzo, an iPhone or iPad on the same Wi-Fi can
[act as its remote](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/): browse the TV's
library on the phone, search with a real keyboard, and start playback on the
big screen with a tap. Pairing runs over your own network, discovered
automatically. The TV does not care what the phone runs.

## The two places people trip

**Expecting the App Store to be full of choices.** It is thinner than Google
Play for this category, and the reason is worth knowing: Apple reviews apps
against a rule that a player must not supply or promote content. A player that
ships as a player — bring your own playlist, nothing inside — passes; the
"catalogue" apps do not last. So the ones you find are, on the whole, the
honest ones.

**Typing a long Xtream password on a phone keyboard.** Paste it. Copy the
credentials from wherever your provider sent them and paste into each field;
a single wrong character in a 24-character password produces
[a link that will not load](/blog/playlist-link-wont-load/) and no clue why.

## Moving between the two

If you already have Vidonzo on Android, signing in on the iPhone brings your
favourites and resume points across; the encrypted playlist credentials
follow once a device that can unlock them approves the new one. The mechanics
are the same as [moving to any new phone](/blog/move-to-a-new-phone/). Nothing about the
platform switch requires setting up twice.

The short version: on an iPhone the playlist is the same, the player is the
same, and the setup is [the same minute-long walkthrough](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/).
What changes is that the app comes from one place, a file is picked from the
Files app, and the phone will still happily drive the TV.
