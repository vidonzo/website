The guide — the grid showing what is on now, what is next, and what was on an
hour ago — is called the **EPG**, for electronic programme guide. On a satellite
box it arrives inside the broadcast signal. In an IPTV player it does not, and
that difference explains almost every strange thing a guide ever does.

## It is a second file, not part of the channel list

Your channel list says *where the streams are*. The EPG says *what is on them and
when*. They are separate documents, fetched separately, updated on different
schedules.

The usual format is **XMLTV**: an XML file listing channels, then programmes with
a channel reference, a start time, an end time,
[a title, and often a description](/blog/watch-in-your-language/).
A guide covering a few hundred channels for a week is a large file — tens of
megabytes is ordinary — which is why a player fetches it in the background rather
than at the moment you press the guide button.

If you use [Xtream Codes](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/), the guide usually comes
along automatically: the API exposes it, and the player collects it without you
configuring anything. With a plain M3U you often supply the EPG URL yourself,
because a playlist file has nowhere to put one.

## How a programme finds its channel

This is where guides break, and the reason is unglamorous: the two files have to
agree on a name.

Each entry in the guide is tagged with a channel identifier. Each channel in your
playlist can carry one too, in the `tvg-id` attribute. The player matches them.
When they match, you get a programme grid. When they do not, you get a channel
that plays perfectly and shows nothing in the guide.

That is the single most common EPG complaint, and it is almost never the player.
`Channel One HD` in the playlist and `channel.one.hd` in the guide are different
strings, and nothing can join them but a correction on the provider's side or a
manual mapping.

## Time zones, the other classic failure

An XMLTV timestamp carries a UTC offset — `20260729180000 +0300`. When a
provider's file omits the offset, or states one that does not match reality, the
whole guide slides: everything is right, just an hour or three away from the
truth.

If your guide is uniformly shifted by a whole number of hours, that is what has
happened, and it is a provider fix rather than a device setting. If only some
channels are shifted, they are being sourced from a different guide feed than the
rest.

## What a good guide gives you

Once it is matching, the EPG stops being decoration:

- **Now and next**, so you can see what you are joining halfway through.
- **A grid you can scroll ahead in**, which is the only comfortable way to plan an
  evening across hundreds of channels.
- **Reminders**, on players that offer them.
- **Better search**, because you can look for a programme rather than a channel.
- **Catch-up**, where your provider supports it: the guide is what gives the
  player something to ask for.

## When the guide is empty

Working through it in order:

1. **Wait.** After adding a playlist, the first EPG fetch takes a while. Nothing
   is wrong for the first few minutes.
2. **Check there is a guide at all.** With an M3U source, if you were never given
   an EPG URL, there is nothing to display. Ask your provider for one.
3. **Check one channel versus all channels.** A few blank channels is an
   identifier mismatch. Every channel blank is a fetch problem.
4. **Refresh the guide** if the player offers it, which re-downloads rather than
   re-reads the cache.
5. **Consider the hour.** Some providers publish a narrow window — only today and
   tomorrow — and it looks empty if you scroll far ahead.

None of these are about playback, which is a useful clue in itself: a channel that
plays but has no guide entry, and a channel that has a guide entry but will not
play, are unrelated problems with unrelated causes — the second one is usually
[the network path](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/).

## Why the player's part of this matters

A guide is a lot of data to hold gracefully. Parsing tens of megabytes of XML,
keeping it current, and drawing a scrollable grid for hundreds of channels
without the interface becoming unusable is real work — and it is the kind of work
[a player is actually responsible for](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/), unlike
the contents of the guide itself.

It is also a place where privacy quietly matters. A guide fetch is a request to
your provider's URL and nowhere else; a player that reports back which programmes
you scrolled past would be collecting a remarkably detailed picture of your
evening for no reason connected to showing you a grid.
