There is a fault pattern that sends people to their router for no reason:
ninety-odd channels play beautifully, and four or five refuse — the same four or
five, every time, at every hour of the day, on Wi-Fi and on mobile data alike.

That consistency is the whole diagnosis. A network problem is a *timing*
problem: it wanders, it is worse in the evening, it affects whatever you happen
to be watching. A fault that follows the same specific channels around, and
disappears when you open them on a different device, is not about the network at
all. It is about decoding.

## The file extension tells you nothing about the video

A stream arrives in a *container* — `.ts`, or an `.m3u8` segment list, or
something a
[protocol wraps around it](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/). The container
is a box. It says how the pieces are arranged and nothing whatsoever about what
is inside them.

Inside are at least two separately-encoded things: a video track and one or more
audio tracks, each in its own *codec*. Your device needs to understand both. It
frequently understands one and not the other, which is why the failures are
weirder than a simple refusal.

## Video codecs, in the order you will meet them

**H.264 (AVC)** is the universal floor. Every phone, tablet and TV box made in
the last fifteen years decodes it in hardware. A channel in H.264 that does not
play has a different problem.

**HEVC (H.265)** is where the trouble starts. It halves the bandwidth for the
same picture, so providers use it for HD and 4K feeds, and support for it is
genuinely uneven. Recent phones handle it in hardware without noticing. Cheap
television boxes often support it at some resolutions and profiles but not
others — a distinction no interface ever explains, which is why a box can play
one HEVC channel and choke on the next.

**MPEG-2** is the legacy case, still carried by feeds that originate from
older broadcast infrastructure. It is ancient and undemanding, and modern mobile
chips have quietly dropped hardware support for it because nothing else needs it
any more.

**AV1** is efficient, newer, and rare in live television. If you meet it, it is
on a very new device or not at all.

## Audio is where the classic failure lives

**AAC** is safe everywhere. **AC-3 and E-AC-3** — Dolby Digital and Digital Plus
— are extremely common on television feeds, because that is what broadcast
carries, and they are the usual explanation for the most confusing symptom of
all: a perfect picture with complete silence.

If a channel plays video with no sound while other channels are fine, do not
look at your volume. Look at whether the stream offers a second audio track;
[switching tracks](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/) frequently lands on an AAC
alternative that plays immediately.

## Reading the symptoms

| What happens | Where it broke |
|---|---|
| Sound plays, picture is black | Video codec unsupported, or the decoder refused it |
| Picture plays, no sound at all | Audio codec — usually AC-3 or E-AC-3 |
| Green, pink or smeared blocks | Hardware decoder given something it half-supports |
| Combed horizontal lines on movement | Interlaced source, not deinterlaced |
| Plays but stutters constantly, device gets hot | Falling back to software decoding |
| Works on the phone, fails on the TV box | The box has the older, narrower decoder set |
| Black screen, then a clean error | Decoder initialisation failed outright |

That last row deserves emphasis, because it is the test that ends most
arguments. **Open the failing channel on a different device.** If it plays there,
nothing is wrong with your playlist, your account or your connection, and no
setting on the first device is going to conjure a decoder its chip does not
have.

## Hardware and software decoding, and the trade

Every device has a fixed-function block that decodes a specific list of formats
at specific resolutions. It is fast, it barely warms the device, and its list is
frozen at manufacture. Anything outside it must be decoded in software on the
CPU — which can handle almost any format ever devised, at a cost in heat, battery
and, past a certain resolution, sheer feasibility. A phone that software-decodes
1080p may be fine. The same phone will not software-decode 4K, and it will make
that clear within seconds.

This is the real explanation behind two things that otherwise look like magic:
why a five-year-old TV box fails on channels a two-year-old phone plays without
comment, and why one player succeeds where another gives up. A player built on a
broad software decoder has somewhere to fall back to. Vidonzo is built on libmpv
for this reason — hardware decoding where the chip can take it, and an engine
behind it that plays what narrower players refuse.

## Interlacing, the artefact nobody names

Some feeds are still 1080**i** rather than 1080p: each frame arrives as two
half-frames from slightly different moments. On anything with movement — a
camera pan, a ball, a crowd — undeinterlaced 1080i shows fine horizontal combing
along the edges of moving objects.

It is not damage, it is not your connection, and it is not the codec. It is a
sixty-year-old broadcast technique meeting a progressive-scan display, and the
fix belongs to the player's deinterlacer rather than to anything you can change
about the stream.

## What no player can fix

Three things are simply outside the app's reach, and it is worth knowing them so
you stop looking:

- **A decoder your chip does not have**, at the resolution the channel needs.
  Software fallback stretches this, but it does not repeal physics.
- **A feed encoded badly at source.** If the provider's encoder is producing a
  broken stream, every player on every device will show the same artefacts.
- **A channel that is simply dead.** A stream that returns nothing is not a
  codec problem; that belongs to
  [the playlist and account diagnosis](/blog/playlist-saved-but-nothing-plays/)
  instead.

The useful boundary is this: **all** channels failing is an account or network
question. **Some** channels failing, consistently, on one device, is a decoding
question — and once you know which of the two you have, the fix stops being a
guess.
