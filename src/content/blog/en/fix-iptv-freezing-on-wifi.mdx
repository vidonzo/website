There is a particular pattern worth recognising: the stream plays perfectly on
the TV wired to the router, stalls on the tablet in the bedroom, and freezes
outright in the kitchen. When trouble follows the *room* rather than the
channel, you are not looking at a server problem or a playlist problem. You are
looking at the radio.

[The general diagnosis chain](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) — server, line, home
network, device — still applies, and it is worth a pass first. This article is
about the link in that chain that fails most often and most confusingly: the
stretch of air between your router and your screen.

## First, prove it is the Wi-Fi

Two minutes of evidence saves an evening of guessing:

1. **Play the same channel next to the router.** If it is smooth there and
   freezes in the far room, the stream is fine and the radio is not.
2. **Run a speed test in the room where it freezes.** Live television needs
   surprisingly little bandwidth — but it needs it *steadily*. A connection that
   swings between fast and nothing will freeze a stream that a slower, steadier
   line would carry without complaint.

If the stream freezes everywhere, including beside the router, go back to the
general chain: the problem is upstream of your home.

## The two bands do different jobs

Most routers broadcast on two frequencies, and they fail in opposite ways.

**2.4 GHz travels far and penetrates walls**, which is why your device clings to
it in the far room — but it is slow, and it is a shared, crowded lane: your
neighbours' networks, Bluetooth, and the microwave all live there. **5 GHz is
fast and clean but short-ranged**; a couple of solid walls can end it.

The freeze pattern this produces is distinctive: the device sits at the edge of
5 GHz coverage, drops to 2.4 GHz, finds it congested, climbs back, and your
stream stalls at each handover. If your router lets you name the two bands
separately, connecting the streaming device firmly to one of them — whichever
is actually usable in that room — often ends the flapping outright.

## Why evenings are worse

Wi-Fi is a shared medium in a way a cable never is. Your 2.4 GHz channel is the
same channel your neighbours use, and at 9pm every household on the street is
streaming at once. The radios take turns; your stream's steady trickle gets
interrupted; the buffer runs dry; the picture freezes.

This is why a stream can be flawless at noon and unwatchable at night on the
same equipment. It is not the provider being oversubscribed — that
[has its own signature](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) — it is the air being
oversubscribed. A 5 GHz connection, which carries much less of the
neighbourhood's traffic, is the usual escape.

## Placement, and the things between

Radio at these frequencies is nearly line-of-sight, and homes are full of
things that absorb it. Each interior wall costs signal; concrete and brick cost
more; mirrors and metal appliances reflect it; an aquarium or a water heater
absorbs it outright — water is remarkably opaque at 2.4 GHz.

The cheap wins, in order: raise the router off the floor, move it out of the
cabinet, and put it near the *middle* of the home rather than wherever the line
enters. A router relocated three metres has fixed more freezing streams than
most settings screens.

## When to stop fighting the radio

Some rooms are simply beyond a single router, and the honest fixes are:

- **A cable, wherever possible.** [For a TV box](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/),
  one Ethernet run ends the conversation permanently.
- **Powerline adapters**, which send the network over your electrical wiring —
  imperfect, but often far better than the far edge of Wi-Fi.
- **A mesh system or a wired access point** in the difficult room, if the
  freezing screen must stay wireless.

## What the player can and cannot do about it

A player cannot smooth over air that goes silent for seconds at a time, but it
determines how the failure feels: how much it buffers ahead, how quickly it
recovers when the radio comes back, whether it rejoins the live stream or
stalls forever on a stale segment. How much *warning* it has also depends on
[how the stream is delivered](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/) — segmented
formats give a player room to read ahead that a continuous feed does not.

What a player should never do is make the problem worse by spending your
bandwidth on [advertising and analytics traffic](/blog/no-ads-no-trackers/)
alongside the video. On a struggling link, every request that is not video is
video you did not get.
