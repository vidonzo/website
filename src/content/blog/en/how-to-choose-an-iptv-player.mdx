Every IPTV player passes the first test: you paste a link or a login, a list
appears, a channel plays. That is why comparing them by screenshots tells you
so little. The differences arrive later — on the third evening, when the guide
is wrong, or the connection dips, or you try to use it from a sofa with a
remote. Here are the ten places where players actually diverge, in roughly the
order they will matter to you.

## 1. Does it take your playlist as it is?

Providers hand out playlists in [two shapes](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/): an
Xtream Codes login (server, username, password) or an M3U/M3U8 link. Some also
give you a file. A player should accept all three without conversion, and it
should let you keep more than one source side by side rather than forcing you
to merge them. If the first screen asks you to reformat anything, that is a
sign of what the rest of the app will be like.

## 2. What engine is behind the picture?

A player is only as good as the decoder underneath it. Ask what it is built on.
Engines with a long history in difficult video — mpv and the FFmpeg family it
sits on — cope with the odd, non-standard streams that providers produce, and
they support hardware decoding so a two-hour film does not cook the phone. A
player that hides its engine is usually wrapping the platform's stock one,
which is fine on tidy streams and brittle on real ones.

## 3. What happens when the connection wobbles?

It will wobble. The question is whether the player reconnects quietly, or dumps
you back to the channel list. Look for automatic recovery, and for a
[cache or buffer size you can adjust](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) — a stable
line wants a small buffer for fast channel changes; a shaky one wants a larger
buffer for continuity. If neither is exposed, the app has decided for you, and
it decided for the average connection rather than yours.

## 4. Is the guide actually right?

Almost every player says it "supports EPG". Fewer parse it correctly.
[The guide is a separate file](/blog/epg-explained/) with its own time zone
information, and a player that ignores that will show tonight's programme an
hour off and call it your provider's fault. Try it at a known time: open a
channel you can verify and check that "now" is now.

## 5. Can you find anything in a large list?

Real playlists are enormous. A player designed around a demo of twelve channels
becomes unusable at real size.
[Search across everything at once](/blog/organize-large-playlists/), favourites
that stay in reach, categories that hold, and — for films and series — posters
and seasons instead of a flat list of file names. This is the difference between
watching and scrolling.

## 6. Does it remember where you were?

Continue watching sounds trivial until it is missing. A resume point per item,
per source, so that stopping an episode on the phone and opening it on the TV
picks up in the right place. If a player forgets, you will keep a notebook.

## 7. Does the television version exist, or is it the phone app stretched?

A TV is driven from three metres away with a d-pad. That needs a
[layout designed for focus and distance](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/):
visible focus rings, a side rail instead of a bottom bar, and every control
reachable without a touchscreen. Bonus points if the phone can act as the
remote — searching and typing on a keyboard in your hand instead of pecking at
letters with arrow keys.

## 8. Who is the player talking to?

A player's job needs one connection: to your provider. Anything else — an ad
network, an analytics service, a "recommendation" backend — is there for the
developer's benefit, not yours. Read the privacy policy for what is collected;
[the honest ones are short](/blog/no-ads-no-trackers/). And ask how your
provider login is stored: it is your subscription, and it should be
[encrypted before it goes anywhere](/blog/why-a-player-should-encrypt-your-credentials/).

## 9. Does it speak your language — properly?

Not just a translated menu. Right-to-left layout for Persian, Arabic and Urdu.
Correct fonts for Devanagari, Bengali and Thai. Subtitle and audio-track
selection that shows the language names, not stream numbers. If you or someone
in the house watches in a second language, this decides whether the app is
usable at all.

## 10. What does it cost, and what does that cost buy?

Free with advertising is a business model, and you are the inventory. A paid
tier that gates *playback* features — hardware decoding, EPG, subtitle
selection — means the free version is a demo. Whatever the price, it should
buy the whole player, and the app should not need to watch you use it to stay
funded.

## Applying the list

You do not need to score anything. Install a candidate, add your real playlist,
and try three things: open the guide at a time you can check, change channels
on the connection you actually have, and find something in your list without
scrolling. Most players fail one of the three within five minutes.
