There is a moment, a day or two after setting up a new player, when people
open the "films" section for the first time and discover what their provider
actually sends: a list. Thousands of lines named things like
`Some.Film.2019.1080p.WEB` and `Show.S03E04`, alphabetical, with no picture,
no synopsis, and no idea which episode you were on. It is easy to conclude
that the provider is poor. Usually the provider is fine. What you are looking
at is the raw material, and the difference between a list and a library is
work the player either does or does not do.

## What actually arrives

It depends on [which shape your playlist takes](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/).

An **Xtream Codes** login gives the player three separate feeds — live, films,
series — and the films and series feeds carry structure: a title, a year, a
poster address, a rating, a plot, a cast list, and for series a hierarchy of
seasons and episodes with an ordering. That is a lot to work with, and a good
player uses all of it.

An **M3U** link is [flatter](/blog/inside-an-m3u-file/): one line per item,
with a name, a group title, and often a logo address. Some providers put the
poster in the logo field and the series name in the group; some do not. A
player working from M3U has to *infer* the structure — recognise that
`Show.S03E04` and `Show.S03E05` are episodes of the same season of the same
show — and it does that with varying enthusiasm.

Either way, nothing arrives *as* a library. The library is built.

## What building it means

Five jobs, in the order they show up on screen.

**Artwork.** A poster is an address in the metadata; the player fetches it,
caches it, sizes it, and does so for every card in view without stalling the
scroll. When posters are blank it is usually because the address is missing or
dead — the provider's metadata pointing at a picture that is no longer
there — and a good player shows a tidy placeholder rather than a broken image.

**Details.** Rating, plot, cast, year, genre — presented on a detail screen so
you can decide whether to watch *before* pressing play, which is the entire
point of a library. Vidonzo lays these out per title, and adds a genre browse
across the whole catalogue for the evenings when you have no idea what you
want.

**Structure.** Series are the hard part. A season is a container, an episode is
an ordered item within it, and "next" has a meaning. The player has to build
that tree from the feed (Xtream) or from names (M3U), then present seasons and
episodes as they are — not as a flat list you scroll to find *S03E04*
somewhere between *S03E03* and *S03E05*. Get this right and *auto-play next
episode* becomes possible, because the player knows what "next" is.

**Memory.** Continue watching is a resume point per item, per source: how far
into which episode of which show, on which provider. It is what turns a
library from a shelf into *your* shelf. Stop on the phone, resume on the TV,
and the position is the same because it belongs to the item and not to the
device.

**Search.** [One field, across live, films and series at once](/blog/organize-large-playlists/),
because you do not know which shelf a title is on and should not have to. Type
a name; the results are grouped by kind; pick one.

## Why the player has to do it

It is fair to ask why the provider does not send a finished library. Because
the provider does not know what screen you are on. A poster grid for a phone,
a side rail for a television and a dense table for a tablet are three
different arrangements of the same data, and only the player knows which one
it is drawing. The feed carries facts; the player turns facts into a shelf you
can browse from a sofa. This is [the whole division of labour](/blog/iptv-player-vs-streaming-apps/):
the streaming service's app is a shop with a screen; the IPTV player is the
screen alone, and it has to be a very good screen.

## What to look for

If your current player shows you file names, the metadata is almost certainly
there and unused. Two quick checks: open a series and see whether it has
seasons; open a film and see whether it has a plot. If neither, the player is
reading the list and stopping. And check that
[the tracks are exposed](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/) — a film with three
audio languages and two subtitle tracks that plays only in the first of each
is a library with the pages glued together.
