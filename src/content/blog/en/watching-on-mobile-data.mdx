Twenty gigabytes feels generous until you watch live television on two
consecutive evenings. Then comes the wrong conclusion: the app must be wasteful.

It is not. What consumes the allowance is the video, and its size was decided
before a single byte reached your phone. There are, however, a few things
genuinely within your control — and several that people turn off hoping to save
data, which change nothing at all.

## One number decides almost everything

A live stream arrives at a roughly fixed rate. Multiply it by how long you
watch, and that is your data. Approximately:

- **Standard definition, around 1.5 Mbps** — about **0.7 GB per hour**.
- **HD, around 3–5 Mbps** — about **1.4–2.3 GB per hour**.
- **Full HD to 4K, 8–20 Mbps** — **3.5 to 9 GB per hour**.

Those figures explain nearly every complaint about data. A ninety-minute match
in Full HD is comfortably five gigabytes, and no setting in any app changes that
arithmetic.

## What the player does not control

This is worth being straight about.
[A player's job](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/) is to connect to an address,
receive the stream and draw the picture. It does not create the video, so it
does not decide how large the video is.

If your provider sends a channel at 8 Mbps, 8 Mbps is what arrives. There is no
"lower the quality" control that can shrink it, because a quality choice only
exists where the provider publishes more than one version.

There is one real lever here: **many providers list the same channel twice, once
in SD and once in HD, as two separate entries.** Look for the same name with a
different suffix in your list. Where it exists, the SD entry can halve your
usage or better — and on a phone-sized screen the difference is far smaller than
you would guess.

## What you can actually change

**Buffer size.** Under **Settings → Playback** there is **Buffer size (cache)**,
30 MB by default. The buffer is data fetched ahead so an unsteady connection
does not immediately show. While you keep watching it costs nothing extra — but
every time you change channel, whatever was fetched ahead and never watched is
discarded. A large buffer suits an unstable connection; a small one wastes less
if you hop around. If the connection itself is the problem,
[the cause usually lies elsewhere](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/).

**The habit of channel-surfing.** This is the least-noticed waste of all. Every
channel you open has to fill a buffer from empty before the picture appears. Ten
minutes of wandering through a list can cost as much as a quarter of an hour of
viewing, without your having actually watched anything. Keeping favourites and
[taming a large list](/blog/organize-large-playlists/) is not only about tidiness
— it cuts data directly.

**Auto-play next episode.** Turn it off if you fall asleep to series. The
feature will keep going obediently until morning.

**The first load of the catalogue.** When a playlist is read for the first time,
the app reads the whole thing and fetches a logo for each channel — and logos are
images, one per entry. On a list of tens of thousands, that is a real one-off
cost. Afterwards everything is cached and reopening the app costs nothing. So
where you can, [set the playlist
up](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/) on Wi-Fi rather than on the move.

**The programme guide.** The EPG is
[a separate document](/blog/epg-explained/) from the channel list, and for a
large provider it can be tens of megabytes per fetch. Not life-changing, but
worth knowing when the allowance is nearly gone.

## What makes no difference

Several things get switched off in hope, to no effect:

- **Hardware decoding.** That is about processor load and how warm the phone
  gets, not data.
- **Subtitle size and colour.** Subtitles already travel inside the same stream.
- **Watching on a tablet instead of a phone.** The stream is the same size;
  [only the screen changes](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/).
- **Which kind of source you use.** An M3U playlist and
  [an Xtream connection](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/) differ only in how the
  channel list is fetched — a few hundred kilobytes. The video is identical.

---

So: manage your viewing time and your choice of channel, not the switches in the
settings menu. An hour of HD is an hour and a half of gigabytes in any app.
What you can genuinely save is the wandering through the list, and a first load
that would be better done on Wi-Fi.
