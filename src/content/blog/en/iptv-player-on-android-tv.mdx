The same playlist, the same streams and the same account behave very differently
once the screen is three metres away and the only input is a remote in someone's
hand. A television app is not a phone app at a larger size. Almost every
interaction has to be reconsidered, and the ones that survive unchanged are the
exceptions.

Here is what actually changes on the big screen, and what does not get better no
matter how the app is built.

## Five inputs against twenty thousand channels

A d-pad has four directions and an OK button. A typical IPTV playlist has
somewhere up to twenty thousand channels. Every other decision follows from that
mismatch.

The phone answer — scroll quickly, and search when scrolling stops being
reasonable — does not transfer, because both halves of it are cheap under a
thumb and expensive under a remote. So the television layout has to do the
narrowing before you arrive: favourites first, then categories, then the full
list as a last resort. If the channel you watch most evenings is two presses
away rather than two hundred, the size of the list behind it stops mattering.
None of that narrowing is invented by the television. The categories are your
provider's and the favourites are your own, both settled
[when the playlist was first added](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/); the big
screen only has to present them in the right order.

## Ten feet, not ten inches

Reading distance is the constraint people underestimate. Text that is
comfortable at arm's length is illegible from a sofa, and a layout that fits
forty rows on a phone should fit a small fraction of that on a television.

There is also no cursor. Nothing can be explained by a hover state, nothing can
be tucked behind a long press that a mouse would have revealed, and there is no
second tap to correct a first one. Whatever an element does has to be legible
from its appearance alone.

## Focus is the interface

On a touchscreen, the thing you are about to act on is the thing under your
finger. On a television it is an abstraction the app has to maintain: exactly
one element is focused, it must always be visible, and pressing a direction must
move focus somewhere predictable. Get that wrong and focus falls into a dead
corner, or vanishes off-screen, and the remote appears to have stopped working.

The related and equally important job is remembering position. Open a category,
watch something, come back, and focus should be on the item you left — not at the
top of a list you now have to traverse again. Most of the felt difference between
a pleasant TV app and an exhausting one is this, not visual design.

## The one genuine advantage: a cable

A television sits still, next to other things that sit still, which makes it the
easiest device in the house to put on wired ethernet. That single change ends
most buffering outright, because
[buffering is almost always a network path problem](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/)
rather than anything the player did, and a cable removes the least reliable
segment of that path.

If a TV box has an ethernet port and the router is reachable, use it. It is a
larger improvement than any setting in any player.

## Installing it: sideloading is ordinary here

Android TV allows applications to be installed from an APK file, and on this
platform that is a normal path rather than a workaround. In practice it means
enabling installation from unknown sources for the file manager you are using,
transferring the APK, and installing it.

It also means two responsibilities land on you. Check where the file came from,
because an APK is executable code and the platform is not vetting it on your
behalf. And updates are manual — a sideloaded application does not receive them
from the store, so you have to return for a new version.

## What the big screen is still bad at

Two honest limits.

**Older boxes can run short of memory.** Cheap Android TV hardware from a few
years ago often has one or two gigabytes of RAM, and a playlist of twenty
thousand entries with a full guide loaded is a real amount of data to hold. A
player can be careful — page the list, keep artwork small, avoid decoding what is
off-screen — but it cannot use memory that is not there. Symptoms are the app
being evicted in the background, or the list stuttering while it scrolls.

**Text entry with a remote is genuinely bad.** An on-screen grid navigated one
letter at a time makes searching a twenty-thousand-channel list something you
avoid rather than something you do. There is no clever fix at the television end;
the fix is
[to type on the device that already has a keyboard](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/).

The big screen is for watching. It was never the right place to make decisions.
