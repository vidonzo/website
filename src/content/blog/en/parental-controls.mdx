Parental controls in a media player are a narrow tool, and the narrowness is the
most useful thing to know about them. They work within their limits and not at
all outside them, and the way they fail is by being trusted for something they
were never capable of.

So it is worth setting out plainly what a player can enforce, what it cannot, and
where exactly the line falls.

## What a player can do

Three things, dependably.

**Lock categories or individual channels behind a PIN.** The unit of control is
whatever unit your list already has: a whole category, or a single channel picked
out by hand. Reaching a locked item requires the code.

**Hide adult categories the provider has labelled.** If the categories arriving
from your provider are marked, a player can act on the marking and keep those
groups out of the interface entirely.

**Ask for the PIN again after a restart.** A control that unlocks once and stays
unlocked until somebody remembers to re-lock it is not really a control.
Re-prompting when the app starts again means the resting state is locked, rather
than whatever the last person to use it happened to leave behind.

## Where the labels come from

How much of the above is available to you is decided by your provider, not by the
player.

Xtream Codes delivers real categories from the server, so whatever grouping and
labelling the provider maintains is what the player sees. An M3U playlist carries
at most a `group-title` on each line, filled in as carefully or as carelessly as
whoever built the file chose —
[the difference between the two formats](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/) shows up
here as clearly as anywhere.

If a provider labels adult categories properly, a rule that hides them works. If
everything arrives in one group called `LIVE`, there is nothing for a rule to
match on, and you are back to locking individual channels by hand.

## What it cannot do

**It cannot classify what nobody has labelled.** A player reads channel names,
category labels and guide data. It does not watch the video. Nothing in it is
examining frames and forming a judgement about them. An unlabelled channel filed
under a general heading is invisible to any rule that works from labels, and a
channel's name is a name — not a rating.

**It cannot know who is holding the remote.** A PIN tests knowledge, not
identity. It separates people who know four digits from people who do not, and a
PIN typed in front of an attentive person is not secret for long. Pick one that
is not also your phone unlock code, and treat it as something that will need
changing at some point.

**It does not stand in for the provider's own controls.** Anything your provider
offers acts on their side, before a stream reaches your household. Anything a
player offers acts on your device, after it has arrived. They are different
layers, and it is reasonable to use both.

**It does not replace a conversation at home.** No setting substitutes for
knowing what is being watched in your own house, and nobody should sell you one
that claims to.

## Hiding and locking are not the same thing

The words get used loosely, so it is worth separating them. Hiding removes
something from view. Locking requires a secret to reach it. An item that is
merely hidden but still reachable through search is tidier rather than
restricted — a perfectly good thing to want, but it belongs with
[favourites and categories as a way of shaping a large list](/blog/organize-large-playlists/),
not with parental controls.

One more practical point: a control is enforced by the app it was set in. A
household running the same playlist on a television and two phones has three
separate surfaces, and how one of them is configured says nothing about the other
two. Whichever devices matter, each needs setting up.

## What it adds up to

A player is a reader of the list you brought it, and
[that is the whole job](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/). It can make parts of
that list harder to reach, and it can do so consistently, quietly, and without
sending anything anywhere. It cannot turn the list into something it is not, and
it cannot supervise a room.

Held to that expectation, it does real work: a category that no longer appears
while someone is browsing, and a code standing between a channel and a curious
evening. Held to a larger one, it will disappoint — which is a reason to
understand the tool, not a reason to skip it.
