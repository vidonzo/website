The Fire TV Stick is probably the most common piece of hardware behind a
television, and its own app store is not where good IPTV players live. That is
not a limitation of the hardware. Underneath the launcher, a Fire TV Stick is
an Android device — Amazon's own build of it, with Amazon's shop where Google
Play would be. Android applications run on it, including a real IPTV player
with a real television interface. Here is what that means in practice, and the
one extra step it costs.

## Why the Stick's own store is thin

Amazon reviews apps for its store, and a player that carries no content of its
own occupies an awkward category there: it does nothing until you give it a
playlist, and reviewers cannot see what you will give it. So the shelf is
short. Meanwhile the players that *are* on the shelf tend to be either the
platform's stock one wrapped in a menu, or apps that quietly bundle a
catalogue — the thing [a player should not do](/blog/bring-your-own-playlist/).

None of that changes what the Stick can run. It only changes how the app gets
onto it.

## Sideloading, explained once

"Sideloading" is the ordinary word for installing an Android application from
its package file — an APK — rather than from a store. On a Fire TV Stick it
takes three steps, and you do them once:

1. **Allow it.** In the Stick's settings, under *My Fire TV → Developer
   options*, turn on installing apps from unknown sources. Amazon hides
   *Developer options* on newer software until you tap the device name in
   *About* several times; that is deliberate friction, not a fault.
2. **Get the file onto the Stick.** The Stick has no browser you would enjoy
   typing into, so people use a small utility from Amazon's own store whose
   only job is to fetch a file from an address you type. Type the address of
   the Android TV APK from the [download page](/download/); the file arrives
   on the Stick.
3. **Install it.** The utility offers to open the package; confirm the
   install. Fire OS files a sideloaded app at the end of your app list
   rather than on the home row, so if Vidonzo is not where you expected it,
   that is where to look — and you can move it to the front from there.

The same three steps are described in more detail, with the general Android TV
case, in [the TV install guide](/help/); the Fire TV variation is only in
where the setting lives.

Two honest cautions. An APK is executable code and the platform is not vetting
it on your behalf, so take the file only from the address the
[download page](/download/) gives — never from a forum, a message, or a
"repository". And a sideloaded application does not update itself; when a
newer build ships, you install it over the old one and everything is kept.

## What you get: a television build, not a phone app

Vidonzo's Android TV build is
[designed for the ten-foot situation](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/): a
side rail instead of a bottom bar, visible focus for every control, and the
whole interface reachable with a directional ring and a centre button.
Nothing in that is Fire-specific. The Fire remote sends the same directional
key codes as any other Android TV remote, and the app asks the system for a
leanback launcher entry while declaring that it needs no touchscreen — which
is what makes it both installable and navigable on a device that has neither
a touch panel nor a pointer.

The player underneath is the same as on every other platform — the mpv engine,
hardware decoding, an adjustable buffer for
[a connection that wobbles](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) — which matters on a
Stick, because it is a small device on Wi-Fi at the back of a television, and
that is [exactly the position where radio links get fussy](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/).

## Skip the remote for the hard parts

The Fire remote is fine for changing channels and hopeless for typing an
Xtream password. Do not type it. Any phone or tablet on the same Wi-Fi can
[act as the Stick's remote](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/): the phone
discovers the TV automatically, and you browse the TV's library, search with a
real keyboard, and start playback on the big screen with a tap. Set up the
playlist on the phone; the Stick shows the picture. That is the arrangement
most households settle into after the first evening.

## The order of operations

Install the app on the Stick first, so it is discoverable on the network. Then
[add your playlist](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/) — from the phone if
you have one nearby, from the remote if you must. Open the guide, pick a
channel, and adjust the buffer if the picture stalls. From that point on the
Stick is just a screen with a good player on it, which is all it ever needed
to be.
