Resolution is the number everyone quotes and the wrong one to reason about. A
1080p stream can be 4 Mbps or 9 Mbps depending on how it was encoded, and those
two cost you very different amounts of data for an identical-looking picture.

The number that actually determines your bill is **bitrate** — how many bits per
second the stream carries. Everything else is downstream of it.

## The arithmetic, in one line

Multiply the bitrate in Mbps by **450** to get megabytes per hour.

That is the whole calculation. A 6 Mbps stream is 2,700 MB — about 2.7 GB — for
every hour it plays. (Using the decimal gigabyte your network operator counts
with, not the binary one your file browser shows.)

| Typical stream | Bitrate | Per hour | Two hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| SD, around 480p | ~1.5 Mbps | ~0.7 GB | ~1.4 GB |
| 720p | ~3 Mbps | ~1.4 GB | ~2.7 GB |
| 1080p, ordinary | ~6 Mbps | ~2.7 GB | ~5.4 GB |
| 1080p, high bitrate | ~8 Mbps | ~3.6 GB | ~7.2 GB |
| 4K | ~20 Mbps | ~9 GB | ~18 GB |

Live channels vary more than this table suggests, particularly upward on sport,
where fast motion forces the encoder to spend more bits. Treat the figures as
the right order of magnitude rather than a quote.

Three hours of 1080p a day is about 8 GB, or roughly **240 GB a month** — which
is why this stops being an abstract question the moment the connection is
metered.

## Why a live stream cannot economise

An on-demand service ships several encodes of the same film and steps down to a
smaller one when your line struggles. That is why streaming apps survive bad
Wi-Fi by going blurry instead of stopping.

A live channel usually has no such ladder. It is one encode, produced once, sent
to everyone. There is nothing smaller to fall back to, so when the line cannot
carry the bitrate the stream does not soften — it
[stalls](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/). This is the most consequential difference
between [a player and a streaming service](/blog/iptv-player-vs-streaming-apps/)
for anyone on a constrained connection, and it is not something the player can
fix. It has only what the feed offers.

Where a provider *does* offer the same channel at more than one quality, that
choice is worth finding. It is the only lever that changes the number
substantially.

## Where the rest of the data goes

Video is over 95% of it. The other components are worth knowing only so you can
stop suspecting them:

- **The programme guide** is a text file, typically a few megabytes compressed,
  fetched once or twice a day. [Its size](/blog/epg-explained/) is irrelevant next
  to twenty minutes of video.
- **Channel logos and artwork** arrive in a burst the first time you browse a
  large list, then sit in the cache. Noticeable once, never again.
- **Advertising and analytics traffic**, in players that carry them, is small in
  absolute terms and worse than its size suggests, because it competes with video
  for a link that is already struggling. [A player with neither](/blog/no-ads-no-trackers/)
  spends every byte on the picture.

## The buffer does not cost you data — but channel-hopping does

A common worry is that a large cache setting consumes more data. It does not. A
buffer reads *ahead* of where you are watching; the same bytes arrive either way,
just earlier. Trading latency for stability is free in data terms, and on an
unstable line it is the single most useful setting there is.

Channel surfing is the expensive habit, and nobody counts it. Every channel
change fetches several seconds of video before the first frame appears, and if
you move on, those seconds are still paid for. Thirty channel changes on a 6 Mbps
stream, at five seconds of pre-buffer each, is a little over 100 MB spent on
video you never saw.

The genuinely large waste, though, is the stream nobody is watching. A player
left running in another room costs 2.7 GB an hour exactly as if you were sitting
in front of it — more data than an evening's deliberate viewing, spent on an
empty room.

## On mobile data

The arithmetic turns brutal quickly. A 20 GB monthly plan holds roughly:

- **7 hours** of 1080p at 6 Mbps
- **14 hours** of 720p
- **just over 2 hours** of 4K

Tethering to a laptop or a television changes nothing about this; the bytes are
the same bytes. And 4K on a phone is the clearest waste in the whole table — a
handset panel cannot resolve the extra detail at arm's length, so you are paying
four times the data for a picture your eyes discard.

Two things genuinely help. Choose the SD or 720p variant where the provider
offers one. And prefer a
[modern codec where you have the choice](/blog/why-some-channels-dont-play/):
HEVC carries roughly the same picture as H.264 at about half the bitrate, which
halves the number in every row above.

## Bandwidth is not the same as data

One last distinction, because conflating the two causes a lot of pointless
worry. **Bandwidth** is how fast your line is — whether the 6 Mbps can arrive at
all, steadily, without interruption. **Data** is the total that accumulates over
the month.

A fibre line has plenty of the first and, usually, no limit on the second. A
mobile connection often has ample speed and a hard ceiling on the total. And a
[weak Wi-Fi link](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/) can fail on the first while
using no more of the second than a perfect one — the stream freezing does not
mean it stopped costing you.

Knowing which of the two is scarce tells you which fix to reach for: a better
radio path, or a smaller number in the table.
