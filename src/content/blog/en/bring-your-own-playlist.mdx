Most apps that put video on your screen are, underneath, a catalogue with a
player attached. The catalogue is the business: it decides what you can watch,
it needs an account to know who you are, and it learns what you watch because
learning that is how the catalogue improves and how the advertising is sold.

A bring-your-own-playlist player inverts that. It ships with nothing. You supply
the list — [Xtream credentials or an M3U link](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/) from a
provider you already pay — and the app's entire job is to read it well. There is
no catalogue, because there is nothing to catalogue.

That is not a marketing position. It is an architecture, and architectures have
consequences you can check.

## What falls away

**No account is needed to watch.** Sign-in exists only to sync your own data
between your own devices. Skip it and the app still works completely, because
there is no server deciding what you may see.

**There is no watch history on a server.** The stream goes from your provider
straight to your device. Nothing about which channel you opened at eleven at
night passes through anyone in the middle — not because of a policy, but because
there is no path for it to travel.

**There is no recommendation engine.** Recommendations are the polite name for a
behavioural model of you. A player that does not choose your content has no
reason to build one.

**There is nothing to monetise by watching you.** No advertising SDK, no
analytics library. The usual argument for them is that they fund the product, and
a player that supplies no content has far less to fund — the money in this picture
is what you already pay your own provider, which has nothing to do with the app.

## What you keep, and where it lives

Bringing your own playlist does not mean living without conveniences. It means
being precise about where they sit.

Favourites, watch progress and settings are yours and are useful across devices,
so they sync in plain form, protected by ordinary per-user access rules. Your
provider credentials are a different class of secret — they *are* your
subscription — so they are
[encrypted on your device before they are stored at all](/blog/why-a-player-should-encrypt-your-credentials/),
and what reaches a server is a block nobody there holds a key for.

Two kinds of data, two treatments, decided by what the data actually is.

## The trade you are making

This model is not free of cost, and it is worth naming the costs plainly.

You have to find and pay a provider yourself; nothing is bundled. If a channel
is missing, or a stream is unreliable, the app cannot fix it — that is a
conversation with your provider. And with no analytics, the people who build the
app find out something is broken when a person writes to them, not when a
dashboard turns red.

In exchange, the app has no commercial reason to know anything about your
evening, and no data to hand over if it were ever asked.

## How to tell whether an app really works this way

The claim is easy to make. A few things are harder to fake:

- **Does it work signed out?** If playback needs an account, something other than
  playback needs the account.
- **What does it talk to?** A bring-your-own player should reach your provider,
  your guide URL, and — only if you enable sync — its own sync service. Nothing
  else.
- **Is the privacy policy a list or a posture?** "We may collect certain
  information to improve your experience" is a posture. An exhaustive list of
  fields is a commitment.
- **How does it hold your credentials?** Storing a provider password in a form
  the vendor can read back undoes most of the rest.

If those hold, the app is a reader of your list rather than an observer of your
viewing —
[which is really all a player needs to be](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/).
