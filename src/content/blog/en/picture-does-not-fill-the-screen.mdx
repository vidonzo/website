A television is a rectangle of a fixed shape, and video arrives in a rectangle
of *some* shape — not always the same one. Whenever the two disagree, the
player has to decide what to do with the difference, and every possible
decision annoys somebody. Bars, cropping, or stretching: pick one. Here is
what each of them is, why the bars are usually the *right* answer, and how to
choose the wrong one on purpose when you prefer it.

## Where the shape comes from

Every stream declares its picture size — 1920×1080, 720×576, and so on — and,
separately, the shape those pixels are meant to be shown at. Most modern
television is 16:9. Older programmes, some regional channels and a lot of
archive material are 4:3, closer to square. Films are often wider still,
2.35:1, and arrive *inside* a 16:9 frame with black bands already baked into
the picture by whoever encoded it.

Your screen, meanwhile, is 16:9 — unless it is a phone held upright, or a
tablet at 4:3, in which case the disagreement runs the other way.

## The three things a player can do

Given a picture of one shape and a screen of another, there are exactly three
honest options, and Vidonzo's *Aspect* button in the player cycles through
them in this order:

**Fit.** Show the whole picture, as large as it will go, at its true shape.
Whatever is left over is filled with black. A 4:3 programme on a 16:9 screen
gets bars at the sides; a wide film gets bars top and bottom. Nothing is lost,
nothing is distorted, and this is the default because it is the only choice
that shows you what the broadcaster actually sent.

**Zoom.** Fill the screen, at the true shape, by enlarging the picture until
the bars disappear — and cropping whatever falls off the edges. A 4:3 picture
loses a strip from the top and bottom; a wide film loses a strip from each
side. Faces stay the right shape, but a caption at the bottom or a score at
the top may go with the strip.

**Stretch.** Fill the screen by changing the picture's shape. Nothing is
cropped, but everything gets wider or taller than it should be: circles
become ovals, people become broader. It is the setting most televisions
default to and most people mistake for "correct" because they have never seen
the alternative side by side.

## Which one you actually want

The honest answer is *fit*, nearly always, and it is worth saying why the bars
are not a fault. They are the player declining to throw away part of the
picture or distort it. Once you notice that the people on a stretched picture
are subtly wide, it becomes hard to un-notice.

But there are two cases where another choice is reasonable, and the button
exists for them:

- **A film with baked-in bands on a wide screen.** The encoder put black bands
  into the 16:9 frame; the film inside is wider than the frame. *Zoom* here
  crops away the bands and nothing of the film, and on a large television
  it is a real improvement.
- **A channel with a broken declaration.** Now and again a stream says it is
  one shape and is plainly another — a squashed 4:3 picture claiming to be
  16:9, or the reverse. There is no right answer for a lie; *stretch* is
  the one that happens to undo it. Try the three and stop at the one where
  a face looks like a face.

## When it is not the player at all

Two things look like an aspect problem and are not.

**Bars on every source.** If the same bars appear on the streaming app, the
console and the tuner, the television has its own picture-size setting —
often called *Screen Fit*, *Just Scan*, *Original* or *16:9* — and it is
overriding everyone. Set it to the one that does not scale, and let each
source decide for itself.

**Bars that arrive with a channel change.** A live channel can switch shape
mid-stream when a programme changes — an old episode after a modern one — and
[what a decoder does with that](/blog/sound-but-no-picture/) is to redraw the
frame at the new size. That is not a fault; it is the picture correctly
following the content. If the picture *stays* wrong afterwards, reopen the
channel.

## Subtitles follow the picture, not the screen

One small detail Vidonzo gets right that is easy to miss: subtitles are
anchored to the *picture*, not to the edges of the screen. On a wide film with
bars, the caption sits over the film's lower edge rather than floating in the
black band below it — the way it would in a cinema. (In zoom and stretch the
picture *is* the screen, so the two coincide.) It is why
[track selection and subtitle styling](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/) are
worth setting once and then forgetting.

## On a television, in a sentence

Fit is right; zoom is for wide films with baked-in bands; stretch is for the
occasional channel that lies about its shape. Cycle the *Aspect* button until
a face looks like a face, and — if the bars are everywhere — check the
television's own picture-size setting before blaming
[the box](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) or the stream.
