Ask in any forum how to open an IPTV playlist and someone will answer "just use
VLC" — and they are not wrong. VLC is one of the most respected pieces of
software ever released: free, open source, on every platform, and capable of
decoding nearly anything you throw at it. If your provider gave you an M3U
file, VLC will open it and play the first channel in seconds. So the honest
question is not *whether* VLC works. It is at what point you notice what it was
not built for.

## What VLC does well, and it does it very well

VLC is a media player in the fullest sense. Open a stream URL, drop in an M3U,
and it plays: HLS, MPEG-TS, RTSP, obscure containers, broken files, all of it.
Its decoder support is legendary and its playback controls are complete —
audio and subtitle tracks, speed, aspect ratio, delay adjustments, filters.
For checking that a stream is alive, or that a link your provider sent is
valid, there is nothing quicker. Many people who use a dedicated IPTV player
keep VLC beside it for exactly this reason.

## How to watch IPTV in VLC, step by step

You need the M3U link or M3U file your provider gave you. VLC does not take an
Xtream Codes login directly; if that is all you have,
[the three fields can be written as an M3U link](/blog/xtream-codes-login-explained/).

1. **On Windows**, choose *Media → Open Network Stream*, paste the M3U link, and
   press *Play*. **On a Mac**, it is *File → Open Network*. For a file saved on
   your computer, use *Open File* instead, or drag it onto the VLC window.
2. **Open the playlist panel** — *View → Playlist* on Windows, *Window →
   Playlist* on a Mac. Every channel in the list appears there.
3. **Double-click a channel** to play it. The next and previous buttons move
   through the list in order.
4. **On a phone**, VLC's mobile app has a network-stream option in its menu that
   takes the same link.

A large list can take a moment to fill the panel. If VLC plays one channel and
then stops, you have probably pasted a single stream address rather than a
playlist — [the M3U8 explainer](/blog/what-is-an-m3u8-link/) shows how to tell
them apart.

## Where the seams appear

An IPTV playlist is not a folder of videos, and this is where a general-purpose
player and a dedicated one part ways. Five places, in the order most people
meet them.

### 1. The list is a list

VLC shows your playlist as what it literally is: a sequence of entries. A
[real M3U carries more than that](/blog/inside-an-m3u-file/) — group titles,
logos, channel identifiers — and a dedicated player uses those attributes to
build categories, favourites and
[search across the whole library at once](/blog/organize-large-playlists/).
Ten channels are fine as a list. A provider's full playlist as a list is
something you scroll for a living.

### 2. There is no guide

VLC has no notion of a programme guide, because a film on your disk does not
have one. An IPTV channel does:
[the EPG is a separate file](/blog/epg-explained/) that a dedicated player
fetches, matches to channels by identifier, and renders as "what is on now,
what is on next". Without it you are changing channels to find out what is
playing — which is television as it was in 1985.

### 3. Films and series are file names

Xtream Codes accounts carry films and series with posters, synopses, seasons
and episodes. VLC receives them as entries named
`Show.Name.S02E07.1080p` and lists them alphabetically. A dedicated player
[reassembles the structure](/blog/films-and-series-as-a-library/): a season
you can browse, an episode that knows which one comes next, and a resume point
that survives closing the app.

### 4. Nothing is remembered per source

Continue watching, favourites, a chosen audio language for a given channel,
[credentials for two providers kept apart](/blog/multiple-playlists-one-app/)
— all of that is state a dedicated player keeps and VLC has no reason to. VLC
remembers recent files. It does not remember that you were forty minutes into
an episode on Tuesday.

### 5. It was not drawn for a television

VLC exists on Android TV and it works, in the sense that video plays. But its
interface is a desktop application's interface, and driving it from a sofa
with a d-pad is an exercise in patience. A dedicated player
[built for the television](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) has focus rings,
a side rail, and — in Vidonzo's case —
[a phone that acts as the remote](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/) so
searching is typing rather than pecking.

## The engine underneath is not the difference

It is worth being precise about one thing, because it is often got wrong: the
*decoding* is not where VLC loses. Vidonzo is built on libmpv, which shares
much of the same lineage — FFmpeg underneath, hardware decoding on top,
[the same protocols](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/) handled at the same
level. When a stream is broken, both players will show you it is broken. The
difference is everything that sits *above* the decoder: what the app
understands about a playlist, a guide, a season, a resume point, and a remote.

## So: can VLC be your IPTV player?

If your list is short, your provider gives you no guide, and you watch on a
laptop, yes — genuinely, and you will not miss much. The moment any of those
three change — a real-size playlist, a guide worth having, a television — you
are asking a superb media player to be a thing it never claimed to be.

The graceful arrangement is to keep both. Use VLC for what it is unmatched at:
opening anything, checking a link, testing a stream. And use
[a player whose entire job is the playlist](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/)
for the evening. Vidonzo takes the same M3U or Xtream login you would have
given VLC, and [the first playlist takes about a minute](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/);
the file you already have is all it needs.
