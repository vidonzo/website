**IPTV stands for Internet Protocol Television: live channels, films and series
delivered over an internet connection instead of an aerial, a satellite dish or
a cable box.** The picture arrives as a stream of data, the same way a web page
or a video call does, and an app on your phone, television or tablet turns it
back into television.

That one-sentence answer hides the part that confuses most people, which is that
"IPTV" names a way of *delivering* television, not a service you sign up to.
Once you see the pieces separately, everything else — playlists, logins, guides,
players — falls into place.

## How it differs from aerial, satellite and cable

Broadcast television sends every channel to everyone at once. Your set picks one
out of the air, and the signal is there whether you watch or not.

IPTV works the other way round. Nothing arrives until your device asks for it.
When you choose a channel, the player requests that one stream from a server,
and the server sends it — usually cut into short segments of a few seconds each,
which the player fetches one after another and stitches back into a continuous
picture. [HLS, the most common protocol](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/),
works exactly like this.

Three practical consequences follow:

- **Your connection is the aerial.** A steady connection gives a steady picture;
  a weak one [shows up as buffering](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/), not as snow.
- **Changing channel is a request, not a retune,** so it takes a moment longer
  than flicking through broadcast channels.
- **Anything can be on demand.** Because every stream is requested, the same
  system can offer live channels, a film library and the programme you missed
  last night.

## The three pieces you need

Every IPTV setup, from a telecom operator's set-top box to an app on a phone,
comes down to the same three pieces.

### 1. A service that carries the channels

Somebody has to run the servers that the streams come from. Many telecom
operators deliver their own TV this way; so do broadcasters and subscription
services. This is the only piece that decides *what* you can watch.

### 2. The details that point a player at it

A service gives you one of two things:

- **An M3U playlist** — a link or a small file listing each channel's name and
  stream address. It is plain text you can read yourself;
  [here is what every line means](/blog/inside-an-m3u-file/).
- **An Xtream Codes login** — a server address, a username and a password, which
  let a player ask the service for its channels, films, series and guide
  directly. [The three fields are explained here](/blog/xtream-codes-login-explained/).

If you are unsure which you have, or which to use when you have both,
[this comparison settles it](/blog/xtream-codes-vs-m3u/).

### 3. A player on a device

The player is the app that reads those details and shows you the result. It
reads the list, fetches the stream you pick, decodes the video, and draws a
usable interface around all of it: categories, favourites, search, a programme
guide. It does not come with channels of its own —
[that is not its job](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/), and it is why the same
player works with any service.

## Live TV, catch-up and on demand

Most IPTV services offer some mix of three things:

- **Live channels**, which play as they are broadcast.
- **Catch-up**, where the service keeps recent programmes for a few days and the
  player can request one from the guide. It only exists where the service
  supports it.
- **Films and series**, delivered through an Xtream login with posters, seasons
  and episodes — which a good player turns into
  [a browsable library rather than a list of file names](/blog/films-and-series-as-a-library/).

Tying the live part together is the **EPG**, the electronic programme guide: a
separate schedule that says what is on each channel now and next.
[How the guide works, and why it sometimes goes blank](/blog/epg-explained/),
is its own short story.

## Which devices can play IPTV

Almost any screen with an internet connection and the right app:

- **Phones and tablets** — Android, iPhone and iPad;
  [a tablet is often the most comfortable screen of all](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/).
- **Android TV and Google TV** televisions and boxes, where
  [a player built for the remote](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) makes the
  difference.
- **Fire TV Stick**, which is Android underneath and
  [runs a proper player with one extra install step](/blog/iptv-player-on-fire-tv-stick/).
- **Samsung and LG smart TVs**, whose own app stores are thin — the
  [setup that works](/blog/samsung-lg-smart-tv-iptv-setup/) uses the TV as the
  screen and puts the player on something else.

## What goes wrong, and where

Because IPTV has three separate pieces, a problem always lives in one of them,
and knowing which saves a lot of guessing:

- **The list will not load** — the details are wrong, or the service cannot be
  reached. [Five distinct causes, five signatures](/blog/playlist-link-wont-load/).
- **The list loads but a channel stutters** — the network path, almost always.
- **The channel plays but the guide is empty** — the guide and the channel list
  disagree about a name.
- **The stream stops after a few seconds** — a
  [disconnect, which is a different fault from buffering](/blog/stream-stops-after-a-few-seconds/).
