Almost everyone reading this is setting up for somebody else: a father who
wants the news from home, a grandmother who does not miss the evening serial,
an uncle living alone in another city. You are the technical one and they are
not, and the problem is not getting them to learn. The problem is arranging
things so there is nothing left to learn — and so it still works that way six
months on. This is the order that holds.

## First decide where they will watch

Most mistakes happen right here: you choose the best device for *you*, not for
them.

- **If it is the television**, an Android TV box or stick plugged into it is
  the simplest world there is: one remote, one app, one screen.
  [Vidonzo's television build](/blog/iptv-player-on-android-tv/) is made for
  exactly this — d-pad control and a clear focus ring on every button.
- **If it is a tablet**, simpler still: [the screen that moves with them](/blog/iptv-on-a-tablet/),
  kitchen to bed, and touch comes more naturally to someone who was never
  comfortable with a remote.
- **If it is a phone**, the same phone they video-call you on is enough. The
  screen is small but familiar, and familiar beats large.

One rule: pick the device they *already* switch on several times a day. A new
device is a new habit, and a new habit is a phone call.

## Do all of it yourself, before you hand it over

Do not sit on their sofa and set it up together; all they learn is that it is
complicated. Finish the setup before the hand-over — remotely, on their phone
while it is with you, or on the device you are bringing them.

**One source, no more.** Whichever the provider gave you — an Xtream login or
an M3U link — [enter it](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/) and stop. If you
have two and want to keep the second as a spare, add it, but leave the main
one active. They should never have to choose between two lists.

**No account needed.** Vidonzo works without signing up, and for someone who
forgets passwords that is a decision, not an omission. Everything they need
lives on the device; there is no login to lose.

**Turn the guide on.** Leave *Show program guide (EPG)* enabled. Knowing what
is on now is the difference between television and a list, and for someone
who grew up with printed schedules it is the most familiar thing on the
screen.

**Leave the refreshing to the app.** In the source's settings, set
*Auto-refresh playlist* to daily. The cached list always opens instantly;
this only decides when it updates in the background. When the provider moves
something, nobody has to do anything — not them, not you.

## Make the list short for them

A provider's full list is too much for anyone, and for someone looking for
"the channel I always watch" it is a wall. Spend one evening
[curating favourites](/blog/organize-large-playlists/): the ten or fifteen
channels they actually watch, the ones they name when they ring. Favourites
stay within reach and sort by most recently added, so the first screen is the
one they want.

If it helps, [hide categories behind a PIN](/blog/parental-controls/) — not
necessarily for a child; sometimes just so a thousand irrelevant categories
are out of sight and cannot be opened by mistake.

## Five minutes of teaching, and no more

When you hand it over, show three things and then stop:

1. **This button opens it.** Put the icon on the first screen of the TV or
   phone, so it is the only thing there.
2. **These are your channels.** Favourites; up and down with the d-pad or a
   finger, confirm to play.
3. **If the picture stops, back out and come back in.** Going back and
   choosing the same channel again fixes almost anything a single evening can
   break — and if it does not, it is not their fault and it is
   [usually the internet line](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/), not the device.

Everything else — settings, search, subtitles — later, if they ask. Whatever
you show in the first session is what they will believe they *have* to know;
show less so they fear less.

## Your phone is their remote

This is the part that changes things for both of you. If the app is on an
Android TV, any phone or tablet on the same Wi-Fi
[can be that television's remote](/blog/turn-your-phone-into-a-tv-remote/): you
see the TV's library on your phone, search with a real keyboard, and a tap
plays it on the big screen. Pairing is automatic and there is no code.

So when you visit, every change — a new channel in favourites, a fresh
password from the provider — is made from the sofa on your own phone, without
taking the remote out of their hand or typing a password with arrow keys. And
between visits, when they ring to say "there is no picture", you know exactly
what their screen looks like, because you arranged it.

## What to leave out on purpose

Any app that needs a "code" or an "activation" from a website to work, and
anything that shows advertising. A full-screen advert for someone who does not
know where to press to dismiss it is the exact moment the television becomes
"broken". Vidonzo [has no ads and no trackers](/blog/no-ads-no-trackers/) —
not as a favour, but because a player's job has no room for them — and for
this audience, that absence is a feature.

## The measure of success

If it still works three months later and you have not had a single call about
it, you arranged it right. The setup itself is [a minute](/blog/set-up-your-first-playlist/);
the rest is choosing less — one device, one source, one short screen of
familiar channels, and an update that takes care of itself.
