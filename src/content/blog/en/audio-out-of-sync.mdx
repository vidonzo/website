Nothing ruins a broadcast faster than lips that finish a sentence before the
sound does. It is also one of the most misdiagnosed faults in streaming,
because a stream has four separate places where picture and sound can come
apart, and the fixes for each are different. Change the wrong thing and the
drift stays exactly where it was. Here are the four, in order from the source
to your ears, and how to tell which one you are looking at.

## First, tell drift from offset

Watch for a minute and ask one question: is the gap *constant* or *growing*?

A constant offset — the sound is always about a third of a second late, and
stays that way — was set before the stream reached you, or by your display.
A growing drift — in sync when the channel opens, a second out after ten
minutes, worse after twenty — is a clock problem, and it points at buffering
or decoding on your side. That single observation halves the list.

## 1. The encoder: it left the server that way

Video and audio are separate tracks
[braided into one stream](/blog/subtitles-and-audio-tracks/), each stamped
with timing information so the player can line them up. If the provider's
encoder stamped them wrongly — the audio path added latency the timestamps do
not account for — every viewer on that channel sees the same constant offset,
on every player, on every device.

The signature: it is one channel, or one provider's channels, and it is the
same on your phone as on the TV. There is nothing on your side to adjust; the
only cure is upstream. Check the channel on a second device to confirm, and
tell the provider which channel and roughly how large the gap is.

## 2. The network: segments arriving out of step

On [HLS and DASH](/blog/streaming-protocols-explained/), a stream is delivered
in short segments, and if segments arrive late the player has to choose
between stalling and pressing on. Pressing on is where drift begins: it may
play what it has, and what it has is not always both tracks in equal measure.
On a raw transport stream the same thing appears as packets dropped under
load, taking timing information with them.

The signature: drift that comes and goes with your connection — worse in the
evening, worse on the far side of the house, gone on a wired box. The fix is
the fix for [buffering](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/): a larger buffer, and a
better [radio link](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/) between the router and
the screen. A larger buffer gives the player room to keep both tracks lined up
through a rough patch instead of improvising.

## 3. The decoder: two clocks, one of them slow

This is the case a player can genuinely do something about. Video is decoded
by one component and audio by another, and the player keeps them together by
comparing their clocks. When the video decoder cannot keep up — a low-powered
box, a software decoder chewing through HEVC, a phone that is warm and
throttling — the picture falls behind the sound. The gap *grows*, and it
resets to zero when you reopen the channel.

The signature: growing drift on one device and not another, and worse on
heavier channels. Three things help, in order:

- **Turn on hardware decoding**, in *Settings → Playback*, if it is off. This
  moves the video work to the chip designed for it, and the video clock stops
  losing the race. On some older boxes hardware decoding is what
  [turns a black screen back into a picture](/blog/sound-but-no-picture/) too.
- **Reopen the stream** when it drifts. Both clocks restart together. It is a
  workaround rather than a fix, but it is a two-second one.
- **Give the device less to do**: close other apps, and if the box is old,
  [the notes on lean setups for weak hardware](/blog/old-android-box-smooth-playback/)
  are all about exactly this.

## 4. The display: the television is late

Modern televisions process the picture — motion smoothing, upscaling, noise
reduction — and every stage of that adds a few milliseconds the sound does
not get. Send audio to a soundbar over one path and video to the panel over
another and the two arrive apart. The gap is constant, and it is the same on
every source: the streaming app, the game console, the broadcast tuner.

The signature: it is everything, not just your IPTV player. Most televisions
have a "game" or "PC" picture mode that turns the processing off, and
soundbars have an audio delay setting for precisely this. Set it once and
every source is fixed.

## What a player honestly cannot do

It is worth being clear about the limit. A player controls the buffer and the
decoder — cases 2 and 3 — and it can keep two tracks in step as they were
stamped. It cannot re-stamp a stream that left the encoder wrong, and it
cannot see how long your television takes to draw a frame. Some players offer
a manual audio-offset slider; it can mask a constant offset on one channel,
and it will not touch a growing drift, because a fixed offset cannot chase a
clock that keeps slipping. Vidonzo does not ship one at present, on the view
that the two things a player *can* fix are better fixed than masked.

## The short version

Constant and everywhere: the television. Constant and one channel: the
encoder — tell the provider. Comes and goes with the connection: the buffer.
Grows until you reopen: the decoder — turn on hardware decoding. Four causes,
one minute of watching to tell them apart.
