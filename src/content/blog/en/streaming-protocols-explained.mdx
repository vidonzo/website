Every stream in your playlist arrives by one of a handful of delivery methods,
and which one it is shapes things you can feel from the sofa: how far behind
live broadcast you are, how quickly a channel starts, and whether a network
hiccup means a stutter or a freeze. You never have to choose a protocol — your
provider did that — but knowing which one is in play turns several mysteries
into mechanics.

## The big idea: parcels, not pipes

Old-fashioned streaming worked like a telephone call: a server and a player
held a connection open and video flowed down it continuously. Modern streaming
mostly works like a courier service instead. The video is chopped into small
segments — a few seconds each — placed on an ordinary web server, and the
player fetches them one after another, the same way a browser fetches images.

That single design choice explains a lot. Parcels can be fetched ahead of time,
so a player can keep a cushion against
[a wobbly network](/blog/fix-iptv-freezing-on-wifi/). Parcels can exist in
several quality levels, so the player can step down gracefully when bandwidth
drops. And parcels pass through firewalls and hotel Wi-Fi untouched, because to
the network they are just web traffic.

The price is delay. If segments are six seconds long and the player holds three
of them, you are watching at least eighteen seconds behind the live moment —
which is why a goal can be celebrated next door before your commentator has
seen the shot.

## HLS: a playlist of pieces

HLS — HTTP Live Streaming — is the most common parcel system, and you have
already met its paperwork:
[the `.m3u8` file is exactly the m3u8 in "M3U vs M3U8"](/blog/m3u-vs-m3u8/). It
is a small text file listing the segments available right now; the player
re-fetches it every few seconds, notices the new segment at the end, and
collects it.

For live channels the playlist rolls forward forever. For catch-up and films it
is a complete list with a beginning and an end, which is why seeking works
there and not on live television. HLS grew up on phones and is everywhere now;
if your stream URL ends in `.m3u8`, this is what you have.

## DASH: the same idea with different paperwork

MPEG-DASH is HLS's international-standard sibling. The segments work the same
way; the difference is the manifest — an XML document rather than a text
playlist — and a certain codec-agnostic neutrality that made it popular with
big broadcasters. From the sofa, DASH and HLS are indistinguishable, which is
itself the lesson: they are two dialects of the same parcel language, and a
good player speaks both without telling you.

## RTSP: the old telephone call, still on the line

RTSP is the surviving member of the pipe family. A player asks the server to
start sending, and video flows over a held-open connection with almost no
delay. That immediacy is why security cameras still speak it, and why a few
IPTV sources do too.

Its weaknesses are the mirror of its strength. There is no cushion of fetched
parcels, so a network hiccup is felt immediately; there are no quality ladders
to step down; and the connection types it uses are exactly the kind that
strict firewalls and shared networks dislike. When an RTSP stream works, it is
impressively live. When it doesn't, there is little a player can do to soften
it.

## MPEG-TS over HTTP: the continuous feed

A large share of live IPTV uses something plainer than any of the above: a
single HTTP connection down which the server pours an unbroken MPEG transport
stream. No manifest, no segments — the same container satellite and cable
television use, poured over the internet. If a live URL ends in `.ts` or has no
telling extension at all, it is often this.

It is simple and it starts fast, but everything the parcel systems offer is
absent: no quality ladder, no re-fetching a missed piece, no seeking backwards.
The player's buffer is the only cushion there is, which is why
[buffer behaviour matters more on live IPTV](/blog/fix-iptv-buffering/) than
anywhere else in streaming.

## What this explains in practice

- **Your stream is behind your neighbour's satellite box.** Segmented delivery
  trades seconds of delay for smoothness, by design.
- **Channels take a moment to start.** The player must fetch a manifest and a
  first parcel or two before it can draw a frame.
- **You cannot rewind live television, but catch-up works.** A rolling live
  playlist has no past to seek into; a complete one does — and catch-up is
  [what the guide gives the player something to ask for](/blog/epg-explained/).
- **One channel freezes hard while others degrade gently.** The gentle one has
  a quality ladder to climb down; the frozen one is probably a raw feed with
  nowhere to go.

## Why a player must speak all of them

A streaming service optimises one delivery path and tests it end to end. An
IPTV player [inherits whatever your provider chose](/blog/what-an-iptv-player-does/)
— segmented HLS on one channel, a raw transport stream on the next, an RTSP
camera feed after that — and is expected to make them all feel like
television. Handling each protocol's failures gracefully, without ceremony and
without asking you to care about any of this, is a fair test of whether a
player deserves the screen it is on.
