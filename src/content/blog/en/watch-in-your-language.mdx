There is a difference between an app that has been translated into your language
and an app that supports it. If you read English, you will rarely meet the gap,
because English is the case every player was built around by default. Everyone
else meets it within seconds, usually without being able to name what is wrong.

A player that treats your language as a first-class case is not doing you a
favour. It is doing ordinary engineering: layout follows the direction of the
script, the typeface matches the writing system, text is shown as the bytes
arrived, and whatever the stream carries is offered to you. The places where
that work gets skipped are always the same three: the layout, the type, and the
tracks that come down with the stream.

## Right-to-left is the sharpest example

Arabic, Persian, Hebrew and Urdu read from the right, and the cheapest way to
claim support for them is to mirror a few icons. The result is an app whose
arrows point the other way but which still thinks from the left.

A real layout starts the whole interface from the right edge:

- **Reading order.** Headings, body text and buttons begin at the right. The
  primary action sits where the eye reaches first, not where a left-to-right
  design happened to leave it.
- **Focus order.** This is where it stops being cosmetic. Press right on a
  television remote and focus should move to the next item in reading order —
  which, in a right-to-left interface, is the item to the left. An app that
  misses this is tolerable on a phone and close to unusable with a d-pad.
- **The direction of back.** A back arrow should point towards where *previous*
  is. In a right-to-left layout, previous is to the right.

Progress bars are the third decision, and the one most often made by accident. A
loading bar in a right-to-left layout fills from the right, like the text. A
playback timeline usually stays left-to-right, because it maps onto the passage
of time rather than reading order, and someone scrubbing with a remote expects
that. The point is not which answer is correct. It is that a player which never
asked the question answered it by chance.

## The typeface has to match the script

When an app does not name a font for a script, the system substitutes whatever it
judges closest. The text stays readable, so nothing looks broken — until you look
at the letters.

For Persian, the substitute is usually an Arabic font, and two letters give it
away: *ye* and *kaf* are drawn differently in the two traditions, so a word
appears with an extra pair of dots or an extra stroke. It is your word, spelled
wrong. Diacritics are dropped or badly positioned, which does not matter in most
channel names and matters a great deal in proper nouns. And the digits are two
separate sets — Persian ۱۲۳۴ and Arabic ١٢٣٤ — so a list that mixes them reads as
foreign immediately.

The same class of failure shows up elsewhere: stacked Vietnamese diacritics that
collide, Japanese text rendered with Chinese glyph forms. The font is not
decoration here. It decides whether the word on screen is the right word.

## Channel names: usually the playlist, not the interface

This is worth saying plainly, because players get blamed for it constantly. When
channel names in a non-Latin script arrive as question marks or a run of
mojibake, the fault is almost always the encoding of the playlist itself rather
than the interface. That is a UTF-8 problem, and it comes back to
[the file extension and what it declares](/blog/m3u-vs-m3u8/). A player can
display the bytes it was given correctly; it cannot reconstruct a name that never
reached it intact.

The other half is what happens once the names do arrive intact. Search has to
accept the script, and the prefixes providers bolt onto the front of a name —
`IR:`, `[HD]`, `FR|` — must not stop you finding it. That is the same work that
[makes a large list navigable at all](/blog/organize-large-playlists/).

## Subtitles and multiple audio tracks

Some channels and most films carry more than one audio track, and sometimes one
or more subtitle tracks. Whatever is there, the provider put it in the stream,
and the label on each track comes from the stream too.

Three things are the player's job:

- **Present them so they can be chosen.** A clear list during playback, not
  something buried in settings, and carrying the labels the stream supplied.
- **Switch without interrupting.** Choosing an audio language or a subtitle track
  should not mean returning to the start of the programme.
- **Remember the choice.** If you have to select your language again every time,
  the feature is half-built.

And one limit that no setting gets around: a player can only offer what is in the
stream. If a provider sends a film with a single audio track and no subtitles, no
option in any player will create a subtitle track that was never sent. If a
particular language matters to you, that is the question to put to your provider
before you renew.

Nothing above is unusual work. Nobody calls any of it *language support* when the
language is English. They just say the app works.
